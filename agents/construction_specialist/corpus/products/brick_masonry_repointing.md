## 1. The governing principle (most important, often violated)

**A basic principle of historic masonry: the mortar must always be softer — weaker and more permeable — than the masonry units it touches** [NPS Preservation Brief 2, "Repointing Mortar Joints in Historic Masonry Buildings"]. The mortar is meant to be the **sacrificial** element: it should crack/erode before the brick does, and it must let the wall dry out.

**Why hard Portland-cement mortar destroys soft (esp. historic) brick:** a mortar harder than the brick won't accommodate the brick's seasonal/thermal expansion-contraction, so stress is forced into the brick — causing the brick faces to **crack and spall** ("turn to powder"). Its lower porosity also traps moisture in the wall, driving **freeze-thaw** damage to the units [NPS Brief 2; Wisconsin Historical Society; restoration-supplier technical guidance]. This is the single most consequential repointing error.

## 2. Mortar types — ASTM C270

**ASTM C270** ("Standard Specification for Mortar for Unit Masonry") defines mortar **Types M, S, N, O, and K**, ranked by compressive strength (**M strongest → O/K weakest**) [ASTM C270]. It allows two alternative specification routes:

- **Proportion specification** — a recipe (fixed cement : lime : sand proportions); no strength testing required.
- **Property specification** — the producer designs the mix to meet performance properties (compressive strength, air content, water retention); not both routes at once.

For repointing, **softer types (N, O, or lime-rich mixes)** are generally appropriate for older/softer brick; high-strength M/S Portland-rich mortars are usually **wrong** for historic soft brick (see §1). **The correct mortar is the one matched to the existing units and the existing historic mortar — confirm by analysis, not by defaulting to a modern bag mix.**

## 3. Lime mortars and historic brick

For masonry built **before WWII**, traditional **lime mortar** is often the right choice — softer, more flexible, more vapor-permeable than modern Portland-rich mixes, so it accommodates movement and lets moisture escape [Wisconsin Historical Society; NPS Brief 2]. Matching the **historic mortar's composition, color, texture, and joint profile** is a stated NPS objective; a good repointing job is intended to last **decades (NPS cites at least ~30 years, ideally 50–100)** [NPS Brief 2].

## 4. Joint preparation (where repointing succeeds or fails)

- **Rake out deteriorated mortar to roughly 2 to 2-1/2 times the joint width** (e.g., a 3/8-in. joint → about 3/4–1 in. deep), back to sound mortar, to give the new mortar enough depth to grip [NPS Brief 2; IMI "Repointing Masonry Walls"; restoration-supplier guidance]. A common practical floor is ~1/2 in. minimum.
- **Cut square, not feathered**; **do not widen or damage the brick edges** — hand tools or small/careful power tools; aggressive grinders that cut into brick faces/arrises are a frequent cause of damage on historic work.
- **Do not over-rake** to where you undermine units.

## 5. Repointing execution

- **Dampen** the joints before filling (so the brick/old mortar doesn't dewater the new mortar), fill in **thin lifts** (compacting each), and **tool the finished joint to match the historic profile** (concave, struck, etc.) and color/texture [NPS Brief 2]. Matching the original joint profile is both performance and appearance.

## 6. Facade / LL11 relevance & wall water management

- Repointing is core facade-restoration scope and a routine FISP item — deteriorated joints admit water that drives interior masonry and embedded-steel deterioration.
- **Wall type matters:** in **cavity walls**, through-wall **flashing and weep holes** manage water that gets behind the veneer; in older **solid (mass) masonry**, the wall manages moisture by absorbing and drying (which is exactly why a vapor-permeable mortar matters — §1/§3). *(Flashing/weep detailing on a given wall is a design-professional call.)*

## 7. Common failure modes

- **Hard mortar spalling soft brick** — the §1 failure: Portland-rich mortar on soft/historic brick → cracked, spalled brick faces.
- **Mortar mismatch** — wrong strength/permeability/composition vs. the existing units and mortar; or color/profile mismatch that fails the preservation intent.
- **Inadequate joint prep** — too shallow a rake, feather-edged or contaminated joints → new mortar debonds and falls out.
- **Brick damage during cutting** — over-aggressive grinding widening joints / chipping arrises.
- **Trapped moisture / freeze-thaw** — low-permeability repointing on a wall that needs to breathe.

## 8. Governing standards & references
- **NPS Preservation Brief 2** — repointing historic masonry (the canonical guidance: softer-than-brick principle, prep, matching).
- **ASTM C270** — mortar for unit masonry (Types M/S/N/O/K; proportion vs property spec).
